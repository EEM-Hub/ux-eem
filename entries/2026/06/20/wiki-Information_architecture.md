## Information Architecture: Structural Design of Shared Information Environments

Information architecture (IA) is the structural design of shared information environments — particularly the organization of websites, intranets, and software — to support usability and findability. Coined by Richard Saul Wurman, the field draws on principles from design, architecture, and information science, and has evolved into a community of practice focused on digital spaces. IA operates across multiple domains: user experience design, information systems, data architecture, and enterprise systems design.

## Key Concepts

- **Core definition**: The combination of organization, labeling, search, and navigation systems within websites and intranets
- **Primary goals**: Support usability and findability of information in complex digital environments
- **Scope varies by discipline**: In UX it emphasizes user experience; in systems design it may be limited strictly to information science applied to web design (classification, retrieval)
- **Subset of data architecture**: IA can be viewed as constructing, designing, and arranging usable data in the fashion most useful to users
- **Enterprise architecture component**: In systems design, IA is the component dealing with information structure within an enterprise
- **Eight principles of IA** (Dan Brown): objects, choices, disclosure, exemplars, front doors, multiple classification, focused navigation, growth

## Commands and Syntax

No commands or configuration syntax — this is a conceptual/disciplinary topic. Key methods and techniques referenced:

- **Card sorting** — eliciting user mental models to inform categorization
- **Tree testing** — evaluating topic hierarchies for findability
- **Site maps** — visual representations of information structure
- **Controlled vocabularies** — standardized labeling schemes to reduce ambiguity
- **Faceted classification** — organizing content along multiple independent dimensions

## Relationships

- **Usability / User Experience Design**: IA is a foundational layer; without sound IA, usability suffers regardless of visual or interaction design quality
- **Content Strategy / Content Management**: IA provides the structural framework into which content is organized and governed
- **Interaction Design**: IA defines *what* users navigate; interaction design defines *how* they navigate it
- **Data Architecture / Enterprise Architecture**: IA is a subset or sibling discipline focused on the user-facing information layer
- **Navigation Design**: Organization, labeling, search, and navigation systems are the four pillars of IA practice
- **Wayfinding**: Physical-world analogue; IA applies wayfinding principles to digital spaces
- **Taxonomy / Ontology / Knowledge Organization**: IA draws heavily on classification theory from library and information science
- **Semantic Web**: IA intersects with metadata standards, linked data, and structured vocabularies (Schema.org, SKOS, Dublin Core)

## Exam-Relevant Points

- **Richard Saul Wurman** coined the term "information architecture"
- **Morville and Rosenfeld** authored the seminal *Information Architecture for the World Wide Web* (the "Polar Bear Book"), defining IA as organization + labeling + search + navigation
- **IA has four systems**: organization, labeling, search, and navigation
- **Eight principles** (objects, choices, disclosure, exemplars, front doors, multiple classification, focused navigation, growth) — know these by name
- **IA supports two primary qualities**: usability and findability
- **Discipline boundaries differ**: UX practitioners include user experience factors; some systems designers restrict IA to classification and retrieval only
- **Card sorting and tree testing** are the primary IA research methods for validating structure
- **IA as subset of data architecture**: a common framing in enterprise contexts
- **The "front doors" principle**: users may enter a site at any page, not just the homepage — IA must account for multiple entry points
