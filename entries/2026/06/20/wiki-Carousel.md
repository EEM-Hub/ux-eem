## Carousels: History, Design, and Cultural Significance

This page covers the carousel (also called merry-go-round or galloper), tracing its evolution from 17th-century European equestrian tournaments through its golden age (1870s–1930s) to modern installations worldwide. It details regional construction styles, key inventors, material transitions, and notable surviving examples.

## Key Concepts

- **Terminology varies by region**: "carousel/carrousel" (North American English), "merry-go-round" (International English), "galloper" (British English)
- **Etymology**: From French *carrousel* meaning "little battle," referring to 17th-century equestrian tournaments where nobility practiced cavalry skills like ring jousting
- **Origin as training device**: Late 17th-century wooden horses suspended from a spinning wheel, powered by a draft horse walking the perimeter, let tournament participants practice ring jousting without tiring their horses
- **Rotation direction convention**: UK carousels typically turn clockwise; North American and mainland European carousels turn counterclockwise
- **Golden age**: 1870s to early 1930s — possibly 10,000 hand-carved wooden carousels built; fewer than 200 complete units survive today
- **Material evolution**: Hand-carved wood → aluminum castings (Great Depression era) → fiberglass castings (later 20th century)
- **Platform carousel ("dobby")**: Mid-19th century innovation — animal figures fixed to a rotating circular floor around a central pole, initially operated manually or by ponies
- **Galloping mechanism**: Frederick Savage (1880) installed gears and offset cranks to give horses their up-and-down motion — the "galloping horse" effect
- **Brass ring dispensers**: Encouraged riders to sit on the outermost row; now very rare
- **Band organs**: Provided looped circus music; common during the golden age but now very rare

## Relationships

- **Amusement park history**: Carousels were among the first purpose-built amusement park attractions and became symbols of the entire amusement industry (IAAPA used a carousel horse logo until 2018)
- **Industrial history**: Connected to agricultural machinery (Frederick Savage transitioned from farm equipment to fairground rides), steam power adoption, and electrification
- **Three American construction styles**:
  - **Coney Island style** (Looff, Brooklyn) — elaborate jeweled saddles, mirrors for light reflection
  - **Philadelphia style** (Dentzel, Philadelphia Toboggan Company) — realistic painted saddles
  - **Country Fair style** (Herschell, Spillman, Parker — North Tonawanda/Kansas) — minimal or no saddles
- **Immigration and industry**: The American carousel industry was built by European immigrants (Danish: Looff; German: Dentzel; Scottish: Herschell)
- **Historic preservation**: National Carousel Association (NCA) tracks and awards historic carousels; many are on the National Register of Historic Places or designated National Historic Landmarks
- **July 25 = National Merry-Go-Round Day** (declared 2014), commemorating William Schneider's 1871 first US carousel patent

## Exam-Relevant Points

- **Oldest operational carousel**: Hanau Carousel (1780, Germany) — oldest standing and operational carousel in the world after 2016 restoration
- **Oldest US platform carousel**: Flying Horses Carousel (1876, Oak Bluffs, Massachusetts) — designated a National Historic Landmark
- **First steam-powered roundabout**: Thomas Bradshaw, ~1861, Bolton, England
- **Galloping mechanism inventor**: Frederick Savage, 1880 — gears and offset cranks on platform carousels
- **First US carousel patent**: William Schneider, July 25, 1871, Davenport, Iowa
- **Tallest carousel**: Carousel Columbia at California's Great America — 101 feet (31 m)
- **Only track carousel with waving motion**: Over-the-Jumps Carousel (1924, Little Rock, Arkansas)
- **First new hand-carved wooden carousel since the 1930s**: Richland Carrousel Park (1991, Mansfield, Ohio)
- **Key innovator for export**: Frederick Savage — his fairground machinery was exported worldwide; he claimed credit for "all the principal novelties" in his 1902 catalogue
