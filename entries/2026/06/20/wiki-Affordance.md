## Affordances in UX and Interaction Design

Affordance is a foundational concept bridging psychology and design. Originally coined by psychologist James J. Gibson (1966) to describe what an environment offers an animal, the term was adapted by Donald Norman (1988) for human-computer interaction to mean the *perceivable* action possibilities of an object. This dual lineage — ecological psychology vs. design practice — creates important distinctions that UX practitioners must understand to apply the concept correctly.

## Key Concepts

- **Gibson's affordance (ecological):** What the environment *offers* an animal — relational, depends on the actor's capabilities and intentions, exists whether perceived or not. A chair affords sitting to an adult but not to an infant.
- **Norman's affordance (design):** Action possibilities that are *readily perceivable* by an actor — depends on physical capabilities, goals, beliefs, and past experience. A softball's shape and weight *suggest* throwing.
- **Signifiers (Norman, 2013 revision):** Norman later distinguished affordances (what actions are possible) from signifiers (what communicates where/how to act). Designers need both. This corrected the overloading of "affordance" in design discourse.
- **Relational nature:** Affordances are not properties of objects alone — they emerge from the relationship between actor and environment. The same stairs afford climbing to an adult but not to a crawling infant.
- **Needs control perception:** Gibson's principle that an actor's current intentions determine which affordances they perceive (selective attention).
- **Gaver's three categories:**
  - **Perceivable affordance** — action possibility exists and is perceived (ideal state)
  - **Hidden affordance** — action possibility exists but is not perceived (missed opportunity)
  - **False affordance** — perceived action possibility that doesn't actually exist (e.g., placebo buttons)
- **Davis's mechanisms and conditions framework:** Technologies don't simply "afford" — they *request, demand, encourage, discourage, refuse,* or *allow* actions, conditioned on the user's perception, dexterity, and cultural/institutional legitimacy. Centralizes power and politics in affordance analysis.
- **Value-rich objects:** Affordances cannot be described in value-neutral physics terms — they inherently carry notions of benefit or injury relative to the actor.
- **Neural priming:** Research (Anderson et al., 2002) shows merely *looking* at an object primes the brain to perform the action it affords.

## Commands and Syntax

Not applicable (theoretical concept). However, affordance analysis can be structured as:

1. **Identify the actor** — Who is the user? What are their capabilities, goals, experience?
2. **Identify the action possibilities** — What can physically/digitally be done with this object/interface?
3. **Evaluate perceivability** — Can the user readily perceive the intended actions? (Perceivable, hidden, or false?)
4. **Apply Davis's mechanism check** — Does the design *request*, *demand*, *encourage*, *discourage*, *refuse*, or *allow* the action?
5. **Check signifiers** — Are there adequate visual/auditory/haptic cues communicating *where* and *how* to act?

## Relationships

- **Signifiers** — Norman's complementary concept; affordances define what's possible, signifiers communicate where to act. Essential pairing for touchscreen/digital design.
- **Discoverability** — Perceivable affordances directly enable discoverability of features.
- **Usability** — Good affordance alignment (perceivable affordances matching actual functions) is a prerequisite for usability.
- **Mental models** — Norman's perceived affordances depend on users' past experience and beliefs, linking directly to mental model theory.
- **Ecological psychology** — Gibson's original framework; the theoretical foundation.
- **Form follows function** — Related design principle; affordances operationalize this by making function perceivable through form.
- **Interaction design / HCI** — Primary application domain in design practice.
- **Activity theory** — Complementary framework for understanding tool-mediated human activity.
- **Embodied cognition / Situated cognition** — Theoretical siblings emphasizing that cognition is shaped by physical interaction with environment.

## Exam-Relevant Points

- **Gibson coined "affordance" (1966/1979)** — defined as what the environment *offers* the animal; exists independent of perception.
- **Norman adapted affordance for HCI (1988)** in *The Design of Everyday Things* — narrowed meaning to *perceived* action possibilities.
- **Norman introduced "signifiers" (2013)** to correct the overloaded use of "affordance" — affordances = what's possible; signifiers = what communicates the action.
- **Three categories (Gaver, 1991):** perceivable, hidden, false. False affordances lead to errors; hidden affordances represent missed design opportunities.
- **Affordances are relational** — they depend on the relationship between actor (capabilities + intentions) and environment, not on object properties alone.
- **Davis framework (2016/2020):** Six mechanisms (request, demand, encourage, discourage, refuse, allow) conditioned on perception, dexterity, and cultural legitimacy. Adds power/politics to affordance analysis.
- **Common confusion:** The term is used inconsistently even in authoritative texts — be precise about whether you mean Gibson's (objective possibilities) or Norman's (perceived possibilities).
- **"Good affordance" as uncountable noun** — colloquial usage meaning easy discoverability, distinct from Gibson's and Norman's formal definitions.
- **Placebo button** is the canonical example of a false affordance.
- **Door knob / handle** is the canonical example of a perceivable affordance (shape communicates function).
