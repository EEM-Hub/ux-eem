## Website Wireframes: Structure, Fidelity, and Design Elements

A website wireframe (also called a page schematic or screen blueprint) is a visual guide representing the skeletal framework of a website. Wireframes arrange elements to accomplish a business objective, depicting page layout, interface elements, and navigational systems without typographic style, color, or graphics. They focus on **what a screen does, not what it looks like**.

## Key Concepts

- **Definition**: A skeletal visual guide showing the arrangement of content, interface elements, and navigation systems on a page
- **Core focus areas**: Range of available functions, relative priority of information/functions, display rules for information types, and effect of different scenarios on display
- **Bridge role**: Wireframes connect information architecture (conceptual structure) to visual design (surface), sitting between high-level structural work (flowcharts, site maps) and screen designs
- **Fidelity levels**:
  - **Low-fidelity**: Rough sketches using rectangles, labels, and placeholder content (lorem ipsum); quick to produce; used in early stages to focus discussion on structure over aesthetics
  - **High-fidelity**: More detailed, closer to actual page design; includes real content, specific typography, image dimensions, grayscale values; used for documentation
- **Static limitation**: Wireframes struggle to represent interactive details (expanding panels, hover effects, carousels) because they are static 2D representations
- **Iterative use**: Wireframes support agile iteration through usability testing — users interact with the wireframe, think aloud or answer questions, and researchers synthesize findings to redesign

## Commands and Syntax

No specific commands. Tools and methods include:
- **Low-fidelity**: Pen and paper, whiteboards, basic digital applications
- **High-fidelity**: Computer software, potentially incorporating Flash animation, HTML, CSS, JavaScript for interactivity
- **Hosted platforms**: Wix, Dorik, Duda provide drag-and-drop layout planning within their builders
- **Annotations**: Adjacent notes explaining behavior, especially useful for low-fidelity sketches

## Relationships

- **Information Architecture** — wireframes make IA tangible; IA provides the conceptual structure wireframes visualize
- **Visual Design** — wireframes feed into visual design; wireframes deliberately exclude visual styling to keep focus on structure
- **Interaction Design** — wireframes represent interaction patterns but struggle with dynamic/interactive behaviors
- **User Research / Usability Testing** — wireframes are tested with users to validate design decisions before high-fidelity investment
- **Page Layout** — the meeting point of information design, navigation design, and interface design; wireframing depicts the relationships among these three
- **Three component areas of wireframes**:
  - **Information design**: Placement and prioritization of information for clear communication
  - **Navigation design**: Screen elements enabling page-to-page movement (global, local, supplementary, contextual, courtesy navigation)
  - **Interface design**: Selection and arrangement of UI elements (buttons, text fields, checkboxes, radio buttons, dropdowns)
- **Stakeholder roles**: Business analysts, UX designers, information architects, interaction designers, graphic designers, developers, and product managers all use wireframes for different purposes

## Exam-Relevant Points

- Wireframes focus on **functionality and behavior**, not visual aesthetics — they deliberately lack color, typography, and graphics
- The three components of a website skeleton plan are **information design, navigation design, and interface design**
- Low-fidelity wireframes use **placeholder content** (lorem ipsum, rectangles for images) and are valued for **speed and early-stage communication**
- High-fidelity wireframes include **real content, specific typography, image dimensions, and grayscale values** but still exclude color
- Wireframing sits **between structural work (flowcharts/site maps) and screen designs** in the design process
- Five types of navigation: **global, local, supplementary, contextual, and courtesy**
- Key limitation: wireframes are **static** and cannot effectively represent interactive elements like hover effects, expanding panels, or carousels
- Wireframes support **agile iteration** through rapid prototyping and usability testing
- Conflict potential: wireframing bridges IA and visual design, and overlapping professional roles can create disagreements about fidelity and ownership
- Recommended practice: **business analysts** who understand user requirements should create initial wireframes, then collaborate with designers to refine them
