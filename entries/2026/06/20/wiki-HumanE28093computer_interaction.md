## Human–Computer Interaction (HCI): Foundations and Scope

Human–Computer Interaction (HCI) is the interdisciplinary field studying how people operate and engage with computer systems. It sits at the intersection of computer science, behavioral sciences, design, media studies, and cognitive psychology. The field focuses on designing, evaluating, and implementing interactive computing systems for human use, with usability and user satisfaction as central concerns. The term was popularized by Card, Newell, and Moran in their 1983 book *The Psychology of Human–Computer Interaction*, though first used by Carlisle in 1975.

## Key Concepts

- **HCI definition (ACM)**: "A discipline concerned with the design, evaluation, and implementation of interactive computing systems for human use and with the study of major phenomena surrounding them."
- **Loop of interaction**: The flow of information between human and computer, encompassing visual, audio, sensor-based, and feedback channels.
- **Interface modalities**: GUI (graphical), CLI (command-line), VUI (voice), and multimodal interfaces each serve different interaction paradigms.
- **Usability**: The central quality attribute HCI seeks to improve — includes learnability, findability, and efficiency of use.
- **User satisfaction (End-User Computing Satisfaction)**: A key outcome metric for HCI quality.
- **Dual-side knowledge**: HCI draws from the machine side (graphics, OS, programming languages) and the human side (communication theory, cognitive psychology, linguistics, social sciences, human factors).
- **Safety-critical design**: Poor HCI design has caused real disasters — Three Mile Island nuclear accident was partly attributed to interface design failures; non-standard aviation instrument layouts caused accidents despite being theoretically superior.
- **Semantic gap**: The mismatch between human and computer understanding of mutual behaviors, addressable through ontology-based approaches.

## Visual, Audio, and Sensor-Based Interaction Channels

- **Visual-based HCI**: Facial expression analysis, body movement tracking, gesture recognition, gaze/eye-movement tracking. Lip-reading can correct speech recognition errors.
- **Audio-based HCI**: Speech recognition, speaker recognition, auditory emotion analysis, human-made noise detection (sighs, laughs), musical interaction.
- **Sensor-based HCI**: Pen input, mouse/keyboard, joysticks, motion-tracking sensors, haptic sensors (robotics, VR, medical surgery), pressure sensors, taste/smell sensors.

## Design Principles and Methodologies

- **Three experimental design principles**:
  1. Early focus on users and tasks — identify who, how many, how often
  2. Empirical measurement — test with real daily users, measure quantitative usability metrics (time, errors, completion rates)
  3. Iterative design — design → test → analyze → repeat until user-friendly

- **Design methodologies**:
  - **Activity Theory**: Defines context of human-computer interactions; action-driven design perspective
  - **User-Centered Design (UCD)**: Users at the center; ethnographic studies inform design; related to participatory design
  - **Principles of UI Design**: Tolerance, simplicity, visibility, affordance, consistency, structure, feedback
  - **Value Sensitive Design (VSD)**: Accounts for direct and indirect stakeholders; iterative with conceptual, empirical, and technical investigations

## Current Research Areas

- **AR/VR/MR/XR**: Augmented, virtual, mixed, and extended reality — adaptive interfaces, spatial computing, cognitive load, AI-driven personalization
- **Human–AI Interaction**: Usability, trust, interpretability of AI systems; explainable AI (XAI); human-in-the-loop decision-making
- **Accessibility**: Inclusive design for disabilities; assistive technologies; universal design benefits all users
- **Social Computing**: Interaction as unit of analysis; humans perceive computer interactions more negatively than human interactions despite identical behavior
- **Affective Computing**: Detecting, processing, and reacting to human emotions via ECG, eye-tracking, face readers
- **Brain–Computer Interfaces (BCI)**: Direct communication between brain and external device; bidirectional information flow
- **Security Interactions (HCISec)**: Usability of security features; poor security UX stems from afterthought design, complexity without wizards, and developers designing their own security UI

## Relationships

- **Cognitive Psychology / Human Factors**: Foundational disciplines informing how users perceive, learn, and remember interface patterns
- **Usability Engineering**: HCI provides the theoretical and empirical basis for usability testing and measurement
- **User Experience (UX) Design**: Modern HCI methodologies (UCD, VSD) directly shape UX practice
- **Accessibility / Universal Design**: A growing subfield ensuring inclusive interaction for users with disabilities
- **AI/ML**: Human-AI interaction is a major frontier — designing for trust, transparency, and explainability
- **Information Security**: HCISec bridges security engineering and usability, a relatively new but critical intersection
- **ACM SIGCHI / CHI Conference**: Primary professional community and venue for HCI research

## Exam-Relevant Points

- HCI was popularized by Card, Newell, and Moran (1983); first use of the term was by Carlisle (1975)
- ACM's definition emphasizes design, evaluation, and implementation of interactive computing systems
- The Three Mile Island accident is the canonical example of HCI failure with catastrophic consequences
- Three design principles: early user focus, empirical measurement, iterative design
- UI design principles: tolerance, simplicity, visibility, affordance, consistency, structure, feedback
- Four major design methodologies: Activity Theory, User-Centered Design, UI Design Principles, Value Sensitive Design
- VSD uniquely considers indirect stakeholders — not just direct users
- The loop of interaction encompasses visual, audio, sensor, and feedback channels
- BCI is distinguished from neuromodulation by supporting bidirectional information flow
- Accessibility design benefits all users, not only those with disabilities
- HCISec is a nascent field compared to HCI; poor security usability often results from security being an afterthought
- Douglas Engelbart's quote challenges ease-of-use as sole criterion: "If ease of use were the only valid criterion, people would stick to tricycles and never try bicycles"
